Abstract

          Ventilation plays an important role in the management of conditions that affect respiratory function by providing alternative ways for sustained breathing support. Manual and mechanical ventilation are two vital methods of providing 
          respiratory support, particularly in individuals who are unable to maintain independent breathing. This article aims to compare the effectiveness of manual and mechanical ventilation and to examine their respective benefits, limitations 
          and applications in respiratory care

Introduction

        
          Breathing is one of the most fundamental functions of human life. When a person is unable to carry out this process independently, medical intervention can b critical to the patients' survival. 
          For centuries, medical professionals have searched forr wayss to assist patients struggling to breathe, leading to the development of manual and mechaniical respiratory suport devices from the simplicity of manually operated 
          bagging devices to the intricacy of computer-controlled ventilation systems.

Analysis and discussion

          
          The need for medical intervention becamee quite evident duriing the outbreak of polio in the early 1900s. In more severe cases, the polio virus can attack the nervous system and paralyse the individual, resulting
          in the indiividual not being ablee to breath independenttly. Consequently, there was a call for urgent development of innovative meedical inventions, ultimately leading to the creation of a respiratory device 
          known as the Iron Lung.

          The iron lung was invented by Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw. It consisted of a huge metal box with bellows with a set of pumps used to draw air into and out of the chamber. The persson would be fully enclosed
          by thee chamber, apart from their head (The Iron Lung, 2018). This chamber used a mechanical breathing method known as Negative Pressure Ventilation (NPV).

          For humans, NPV is the normal physiologicaal way that we breathe. Neegative pressure ventilators work by lowering the air pressure around part of your body, making the pressure 'negativee'. This creates a vacuum 
          effect that expands your lungs and chest, thus, pulling air into your lungs. the machine then removes the lower pressure to let your lungs and chest contract, helping you exhale (Starkman, 2025). This process 
          reequires an interface (cconnection) betweeen thee negative pressure geneerator (e.g., the Iron Lung) and the patient (Weiner eet al., 2005,  pp 208-222).

          Unfortunately, at the time, such intricatte maachinery was not made available to every hospitaal in the world, at least not in large quantities. For example, In the early 1950s, Copenhagen was hit with Bulbar Polio. The
          faciilities in the infectious disease hospitals were completely overwhelmed, highlighting the urgent need for an effective means of respiratory support (West, no date).

          Physician Albert Bower and Engineer Vivian Ray Bennett inventetd a Positive Pressuree Ventilator which inflated the lungs through a tracheostomy (a surgical procedure that creates an opening through the front of the neck into
          the trachea to insert a breathing tube). In 195, the Bower-Benneett team published their results in an obsure meedical journal. The article went unnoticed by many, except Danish anaesthesiologist, Dr. Bjorn Aage Ibsen. Having learnt how
          to manually ventilate patients using aa bag-valve mask, IIbsen immediately understood the article's significancee. He then went on to meet with senior physicians on August 25, 1952, where he arguued that positive pressure ventilation
          was the key to medical breakthrough (Wertheim, 2020).

          A rubber bag filledd with an oxygen supply would be attached tot teh tracheostomy tube. Every time tehe rubber bag was squeezed, the patient's lungs would fill up with air. The lungs, however, were agitated and drowned in mucus. This meant
          that the anaesthesiologist wouldd have to administer a barbiturate (nervous system depressant) to continue bagging. Though this was the furthest that medical ventilation had advancedd at the time, this was a very impractical method as
          the doctor would have to keeep bagging the patient in order for them tto stay alive and the process consumed 250 10-litre breathing gas cylinders each day (Wertheim, 2020). The tracheostomy tube and rubber bag did, however, decreease the 
          mortality of bulbar polio significantly.

          Despite the inevitable consequences of prolonged illneess, most of the victims weaned from ventilation as their muscle strength improved (Wertheim, 2020)

          Shortly after Ibsen's breakthrough, in 1954, there was an important shift from manual to mechanical positive pressure ventilation. Swedish physician Cral Gunnar Engstrom developed a ventilator which was able to deliver controlled volumes
          of air at a set frequency, and deliver inhalation anaesthetics, using bith positive and negative pressure ventilation (Wood Library Museum, no date). This provided more consistent and sustained support.

          Subsequent developments, including the Bird Mark 7 and the Siemens-Elma Servo, improved the delivery and control of positive pressure ventilation. This estabished this type of ventilation as a vital part of modern inteensive care.

          Although positive pressure ventilation was necessary in the advancement of mechanical ventilation, most of its methods were invasive as they delivered positive-pressure gas directly into the patient's lungs via an endotracheal (through the 
          nose or mouth) tube or tracheostomy (through the neeck) tube. This method can be associated with complications such as ventilator-induced lung injury. These factors lead to the resurgence of negative pressure ventilation.

          Modern neagtive pressure ventilators (specifically cuirass ventilators) prioritise patient care and non-invasive treatment, while trying to make the devices compact and easy to use.

          Biphasic (two phase) cuirass ventilation uses both positive and negative ventilation to assist in both inspiration (breathing in) and expiration (breathing out). This is done by placing a shell (the cuirass) with an airtight seal on the front part of the
          patient's body, between their collar bone and their upper abdomen (Kristensen, et al. 2023). The device mimics the normal physiological way that humans breathe by lowering the pressure inside the shell around the chest. This expands the chest and generates
          a lower (negative) pressure inside the chest cavity than outside. The pressure inside the lungs is now lower than the atmospheric pressure, causing the air to rush into the lungs (inspiration) (EBME, 2012). The dveice then raises the pressure inside the shell
          around the chest (positive pressure). This then compresses the chest wall and promotes expiration. Unlike the previously discussed positive pressure ventilation, the pressure is provided externally rather than directly into the airways.

          From the Iron Lung to Ibsen's pioneering discovery to modern innovations such as biphasic cuirass ventilation, the evolution of medical ventilation demonstrates the remarkable capacity of medical sscience to adapt and advance, continually redifining the
          possibilities of respiratory care.
